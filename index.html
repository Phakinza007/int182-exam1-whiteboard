4 · Kinds of learning, and kinds of task

Supervised learning
Learning with a teacher — uses labels, there must be a y. Its tasks are classification and regression. Examples: Decision Tree, KNN, Naive Bayes, SVM, Logistic Regression, Random Forest, Linear Regression.
Unsupervised learning
No labels and no y. Its tasks are clustering, association rules and anomaly detection. Examples: K-means, DBSCAN, BIRCH, Apriori.
Reinforcement learning
Learning by trial and error with a reward — points for doing well, a penalty for badly. Examples: chess, Go, a robot learning to walk. In Gen AI it is the 👍👎 buttons.
Classification
The target is categorical — predicting a category. It is predictive.
Regression
The target is numeric — predicting a value, like a car or house price. It is predictive. That sentence opens the Decision Tree deck and can be asked verbatim.
Predictive vs Descriptive
Predictive predicts one variable from the rest — the predicted one is the target/dependent, the rest are predictors/independent. Descriptive finds relationships to understand the structure, without predicting. Classification + regression = predictive; clustering + association rules = descriptive.
AI ⊃ ML ⊃ DL, and DS
AI is broadest → ML inside it → DL mimics the brain with artificial neural networks. DS is a subset of none of them — it borrows all of it and applies it to real data in order to act.

5 · Types of data and scales of measurement

Categorical (Qualitative)
Splits into Nominal and Ordinal.
Numerical (Quantitative)
Splits into Discrete (step values — population, coins in a pocket) and Continuous (no step limit — weight, height, water in a cup).
Nominal
Identifies a category only — numbers can be assigned but not compared mathematically. Gender, blood type, race, eye colour, zip code, brand. An athlete's shirt number (8, 10, 23) is nominal — only a symbol, saying nothing about who is better.
Ordinal
Has an order, comparable but not straightforwardly. Hotel stars 1–5, education level, grades A–F, satisfaction rating, socio-economic status.
Interval
No true zero. Differences are meaningful, and equal differences mean equal distances. °C, °F, pH, TOEFL score, AD/BE year.
Ratio
Has a true zero, so "how many times" is meaningful. Weight, length, pulse, dose, concentration, Kelvin.
True zero
Zero really is the complete absence of the quantity — 0 kg is no weight; 0 K is no thermal energy (= −273.15 °C). 0 °C is not a true zero — heat is still present; it is a number people agreed on. → 50 °C is not twice as hot as 25 °C, but 100 K is twice 50 K.

6 · EDA and descriptive statistics

EDA
Exploratory Data Analysis — understanding the data by summarizing its main characteristics, usually through statistical graphics and visualization. Non-parametric — no assumption about the distribution. It is visualization + statistical summary + hypothesis testing, done iteratively.
Univariate / Bivariate / Multivariate
1 variable (box plot) · 2 (scatter plot) · 3 or more.
Box plot
Shows dispersion (spread), skewness, the quartiles, the outliers — and it compares groups. He said he asks "what is it for?" directly.
Five-number summary
min, Q1, Q2, Q3, max — know all five and you can draw the box.
Q1 / Q2 / Q3 · IQR
Q is for quartile. Q1 is the 25th percentile, Q2 = the median at the 50th, Q3 the 75th. IQR = Q3 − Q1, the width of the box. Each section holds 25%. Q2 is inside the box but need not be centred.
Fence · Outlier · Whisker
Fences at Q1 − 1.5×IQR and Q3 + 1.5×IQR (some use 3, but 1.5 is commoner). Outside a fence is an outlier. A whisker ends on the furthest real data point still inside the fence, not at the fence → the two need not be equal. min/max count only points inside.
Mean / Median / Mode
Mean takes every point into account but is pulled by outliers. Median is the middle once sorted = Q2. Mode is the most common value = the peak of a histogram (its y axis is frequency). The slide stresses that using just one is a bad idea.
Skew
Positive skew — extremes on the right drag the values right. Negative skew — dragged left. The mean is always pulled towards the tail, with the median between mode and mean. In a normal distribution all three coincide.
Scatter plot
Plots two variables against each other to reveal the relationship — together, opposite, or none. He also asks "why plot at all?" — because a table of numbers hides the pattern.
Correlation
Runs −1 to 1. 0 = none. Positive = same direction, negative = opposite. Pearson for numeric and linear; Spearman for ordinal. Correlation is not causation. The formula is not examined.

7 · Modeling — the four most confusable words

Modeling
The broadest — creating a simplified representation of a system or process, via equations, simulations or a physical model.
Data modeling
A conceptual representation of data — to understand it, communicate it, build systems on it. Includes ERD, DFD, UML.
Data fitting
Finding the mathematical function that best describes the existing data. Least squares regression, polynomial fitting, spline interpolation.
Model fitting
Focused on the model — tuning the parameters of a pre-selected one. The emphasis is generalization, working on unseen data. Gradient descent, maximum likelihood. The nesting: Modeling ⊃ Data modeling ⊃ (Data fitting, Model fitting).
Model parameter
Estimated from the data by the algorithm itself — weights, coefficients, the intercept.
Hyperparameter
Set manually by a person, used in estimating the parameters — k in K-means, k in KNN, learning rate, layers, epochs, the loss function. He said he will ask how these two differ.
Overfitting
The model is too complex and learns the noise too — excellent on training data, poor on new. A suit cut for one person that fits nobody else — not general.
Underfitting
The model is too simple to capture the patterns, or there is too little data or training. A suit that is too loose.
Noise · Validation
Noise is the part of the data that arrives unwanted and serves no purpose, but which the model learns anyway. The fix for overfitting is validation.
Data bias / Human bias / Algorithmic bias
Bias is error that creeps in, often unintentionally, out of habit. Data bias lives in the data — e.g. unequal scales making the model lean. Algorithmic bias comes from training. He asks "what kinds are there" and "how do you reduce it".

8 · Association rules

Antecedent / Consequent
In X → Y, X on the left is the antecedent and Y on the right is the consequent. X and Y must be disjoint — no shared items.
MBA
Market Basket Analysis — finding what gets bought together. The classic example is beer & diapers. Association rule mining is a synonym.
Transactional data
Data from one single event. Stored as a set, so no duplicates and no order. MBA ignores quantities — only what co-occurs with what.
Item / Itemset / Frequent itemset
Item = one product type. Itemset = a group appearing together (1-itemset, 2-itemset, …). Frequent itemset = one whose support is ≥ the minimum support threshold. A 1-itemset cannot make a rule — you need at least 2 items.
Frequency vs Support
Frequency is the raw count, undivided. Support is that frequency divided by N.
Support
Frequency(X,Y) ÷ N — "how popular is this itemset?" The one formula he confirmed is on the paper.
Confidence
Frequency(X,Y) ÷ Frequency(X) — "given X, how often is Y there?" From conditional probability. Not symmetric — same numerator, different denominator.
Lift
Support(X,Y) ÷ [Support(X) × Support(Y)] — "stronger than chance?" >1 a real association, =1 independent, <1 negative. Lift is symmetric, unlike confidence. Slide 6 mistypes this.
Apriori property
Part 1: frequent → all subsets frequent. Part 2 (the contrapositive): infrequent → all supersets infrequent — part 2 is what makes pruning possible.
Pruning
Throwing away an infrequent itemset and never expanding it again. Necessary because the power set has size 2^n — n = 1,000 gives 2^1000, which is not computable.

9 · Decision tree

Root / Decision / Leaf node
Root = the first node, the most important question. Decision node = a question in the middle. Leaf = a terminal node, a label not a question. Branch = the line out of a node, i.e. the answer.
Entropy
Measures how messy or impure a group is. Synonyms: disorder · messy · impure · randomness · uncertainty. 0 = perfectly pure, the best · 1 = maximum mess, a perfect 50/50 split. The formula is not examined — the concept is enough.
Information Gain (IG)
Entropy(parent) − Weighted Entropy(children) — how much a question reduces the messiness. It must be weighted because each child holds a different number of points. More is better → the highest IG becomes the root.
Stopping condition
When to stop splitting — a pure node (entropy 0), max depth, minimum samples. Without one it splits until a single point remains, which is overfitting.
Rule-based
Works on if–then. Both decision trees and association rules are rule-based.
Binary / Multiclass
Binary = two ways (the simplest default). Ternary = three. More than that is multiclass classification.
Random Forest
Uses hundreds of trees and takes the majority vote. Each must be different — diversity is the point.

10 · K-means and clustering

Clustering
Grouping similar points without prior knowledge of the labels. Also cluster analysis. It cannot be scored right or wrong — with no labels you can only ask whether it is good.
K
The number of clusters you want. A hyperparameter the user chooses in advance — not something the model learns.
Centroid
A cluster's centre point. Related: center, CG (center of gravity), COM (center of mass). A triangle's centroid is the intersection of its medians.
Euclidean distance
√[(x₁−x₂)² + (y₁−y₂)²], straight from Pythagoras. Measures nearness — closer means more alike.
WCSS
Within-Cluster Sum of Squares — the squared distances from every point to its centroid, added up. Squared to avoid a slow square root, since we only need to compare near from far, not a precise number.
Elbow method
Try k = 1, 2, 3, … and plot WCSS against k. Take the point where the curve levels off, not where WCSS is highest (it falls with k regardless). The alternative is the Silhouette Score, not covered yet.
Non-deterministic
Repeat runs on the same data can differ, because the starting centroids are random. It is also sensitive to initial placement and can converge to a poor solution.
K-modes / K-prototypes
K-means is for numerical data → Euclidean distance → uses the mean. K-modes is for categorical → counts mismatches (like Hamming distance) → uses the mode. K-prototypes combines both; its centre is a prototype.
Hard vs Soft clustering
Hard = one point, exactly one cluster (K-means). Soft/fuzzy = one point across several by weight (Fuzzy C-means). The C stands for class/category — the same meaning as K.
Normalization / Standardization
Normalization squeezes into 0 to 1. Standardization (Z-score) uses the standard deviation. Necessary because unequal scales make the model lean towards large-valued features — which counts as data bias. Normalize the features only, never the target.
Preprocessing
Whatever is done to the data before the next stage — resizing a 1000×1000 image to 500×500, or scaling so values are comparable.

SET 2

EDA and Model Fitting

Class 3 The set with the most confusable definitions — and the one he named the most topics from: box plot, scatter plot, EDA, predictive vs descriptive, parameter vs hyperparameter, OLTP/OLAP, data types.

What EDA is

Exploratory Data Analysis = a technique for understanding your data by summarizing its main characteristics, usually through statistical graphics and visualization.

EDA is non-parametric — it makes no assumptions about the underlying distribution.

It takes in data visualization + statistical summaries + hypothesis testing, and is iterative — not done once and finished.

The trap that could show up: a box plot is EDA, but linear regression is not — it is data modeling. The slide says so outright.

How many variables at once

Univariate = 1 variable — a box plot (one axis).

Bivariate = 2 — a scatter plot (x and y).

Multivariate = 3 or more — predicting price from horsepower + engine size + brand.

The four confusable words

Modeling (broadest) — creating a simplified representation of a system or process: equations, simulations, or a physical model.

Data modeling — a conceptual representation of data, to understand it, communicate it and build systems on it. Includes ERD, DFD, UML.

Data fitting — finding the mathematical function that best describes the existing data. Techniques: least squares regression, polynomial fitting, spline interpolation.

Model fitting — focused on the model itself, tuning the parameters of a pre-selected model. The emphasis is generalization — working on unseen data. Techniques: gradient descent, maximum likelihood.

The nesting: Modeling ⊃ Data modeling ⊃ (Data fitting, Model fitting).

Parameter vs hyperparameter

A model parameter is estimated from the data by the algorithm itself — weights, coefficients.

A model hyperparameter is set manually by a person, used in the process of estimating the parameters — k in KNN, k in K-means, learning rate, number of layers, epochs, the loss function.

This is where open weight model, heard so often around generative AI, comes from. A straight line needs only a slope and an intercept; a modern model holds billions or trillions of parameters.

Underfitting vs overfitting

Underfitting — the model is too simple to capture the underlying patterns, or there is too little data or too little training.

Overfitting — the model is too complex and learns the noise as well: excellent on training data, poor on new data.

Noise is the part of the data that arrives unwanted and serves no purpose, but which the model learns anyway. The fix for overfitting is validation.

His tailoring analogy: overfitting is a suit cut exactly for one person that fits nobody else — not general. Underfitting is a suit that is too loose.

Predictive vs descriptive

The models produced by the data mining step fall into two types.

Predictive — predict one variable's value from the rest. The one predicted is the dependent or target variable; the rest are independent or predictor variables.

Descriptive — identify the relationships between variables in order to learn about the structure of the data. It does not predict.

Example: "this make, a sedan, this much horsepower, black — roughly what price?" is predictive; a descriptive model simply reports what it found. Clustering and association rules are descriptive; classification and regression are predictive.

What a box plot is for — asked directly

Dispersion (spread) · skewness, if any · the quartiles · the outliers, if any · and it compares groups side by side.

Its advantage over a histogram: you see the distribution without plotting everything, and several groups fit side by side in one picture.

What a scatter plot is for — asked directly

It plots two variables against each other (bivariate) to reveal the relationship between them — moving together, moving oppositely, or not related at all.

He also asks "why do we plot at all?" — because you cannot see a pattern in a table of numbers, but you see it at once in a plot.

The three measures of centre

Mean — the familiar average. It takes every data point into account but is pulled by outliers.

Median — the middle value once sorted = Q2.

Mode — the most common value = the peak of a histogram (its y axis is frequency).

The slide stresses that no single one is best, but using just one is a bad idea.

The shape of a distribution

Normal — symmetric, bell-shaped. Mean, median and mode all land on the same point.

Positive skew — extreme values on the right drag the values right. Negative skew — dragged left.

The mean is always pulled towards the tail, with the median between the mode and the mean. Most people are 1.7–1.8 m tall, so a few over 2 m drag the average right.

Correlation

Runs from −1 to 1. 0 = no correlation. Positive = same direction; negative = opposite.

Pearson for numeric, linear relationships. Spearman for ordinal. This course uses Pearson.

Correlation does not mean causation — a sentence that can be asked as-is. And the formula is not examined.

Linear regression and reading the output

Choose the line with the smallest total squared error, by least squares. Of the hundreds of lines that could pass through the data, one is best.

"Regression" translates as "receding" — the error recedes until it is as small as it goes.

The constraint: y must always be numeric, while x may mix numeric and categorical.

Association Rules — Market Basket Analysis

Class 4 + the class 5 refill The only set with a formula on the paper, and the only one you may have to compute by hand. It is unsupervised and descriptive, and rule-based like a decision tree. He said twice: you must be able to write the support and confidence formulas yourself — the paper will not supply them.
1 · What a rule looks like

The if–then form

X → Y reads "if X is present, Y is likely to be present too".

X on the left is the antecedent — a set of one or more items.

Y on the right is the consequent — a set of one or more items.

X and Y must be disjoint — they may not share a single item.

The shapes a rule can take (slide 16): A ∧ B → C · A ∧ C → B · B → A ∧ C. The symbol ∧, or a comma, means "and".

MBA, and the beer & diapers story

Market Basket Analysis (MBA) is the data mining technique for finding relationships between items frequently bought together — uncovering hidden patterns in transactional data and writing them as association rules.

His memory hook: MBA is the same acronym as the Master of Business Administration that everyone used to flock to. Here it means Market Basket Analysis.

Beer & diapers: American transaction data showed that men shopping on a Friday evening who bought beer also tended to buy baby diapers — married men with small children, picking something up for the child while grabbing beer for the game. The assumption had been that diapers were a mother's purchase. It is the classic example of a pattern that is hidden and completely unexpected, which is exactly what MBA exists to dig up.

Transactional data

Information recorded from an individual event — its key characteristic is capturing the details of one specific, single occurrence.

Real data records more: who bought, what, how many, how much they paid, where and at what time.

The timestamp matters a great deal, because mining large volumes has to separate last year from last month from last week. Without one there is no way to tell them apart.

But within MBA we ignore quantities — only what co-occurs with what. Because each basket is stored as a set, items never repeat (three cartons of milk still count as Milk once) and order does not matter.

Item · itemset · frequent itemset

An item is one product type — Milk, Bread, Butter, Eggs, Diapers.

An itemset is one or more items appearing together in a single transaction — a 1-itemset like {Milk}, a 2-itemset like {Milk, Bread}, a 3-itemset like {Milk, Bread, Butter}.

A frequent itemset is one whose support is greater than or equal to the user-defined minimum support threshold.

Frequency is the raw count, not divided by anything. Support is that frequency divided by N. A 1-itemset cannot produce a rule, because a single product type says nothing about its relationship to anything. In practice 3–4 itemsets is about right; past 5 it gets confusing, patterns are hard to find, and it stops being useful.

2 · The three formulas — the frequency version is easier to recall

Slide 7 restates all three in terms of frequency (raw counts) rather than support — easier by hand, because the N cancels. He recommended memorising this version, and the top two first.

FormulaHow it is writtenIt answers
SupportFrequency(X, Y) ÷ N"How popular is this itemset?" It ranges 0% to 100%. Anything below the minimum support threshold is infrequent and gets discarded.
ConfidenceFrequency(X, Y) ÷ Frequency(X)"Given X, how often is Y there too?" His hook: confidence is a condition — "given X, will Y happen?" — so X has to happen first, which is why X is the denominator.
LiftSupport(X,Y) ÷ [Support(X) × Support(Y)]"Is this stronger than chance?" Also written Confidence(X→Y) ÷ Support(Y). Its range is [0, +∞).

Where confidence comes from: straight from conditional probability — P(B|A) = P(A ∩ B) / P(A). Extending to three items: going from X → Y to X → Y ∧ Z just means adding Z everywhere Y appears, e.g. Confidence(A,B → C) = Support(A,B,C) ÷ Support(A,B).

3 · Apriori — why you must prune early

The Apriori property — two parts

  • Part 1: if an itemset is frequent → all of its subsets must be frequent too. If {Bread, Butter, Milk} is bought together often, then {Bread, Butter}, {Butter, Milk}, {Bread, Milk}, {Bread}, {Butter} and {Milk} must all be frequent.
  • Part 2 (the contrapositive): if an itemset is infrequent → all of its supersets are infrequent. Find that {Beer, Chips} is not frequent, and you can prune every larger itemset containing it at once.
  • He tied it back to Discrete Mathematics: part 2 is exactly the contrapositive of part 1 — p → q is equivalent to ¬q → ¬p. There is almost nothing new to memorise; part 2 is part 1 with the sides swapped.

Why pruning is necessary

  • A real shop stocks thousands of product types.
  • With n items, the total number of subsets (the power set) is 2^n.
  • With n = 1,000 that is 2^1000, which is simply not computable.
  • Apriori uses part 2 to cut it down: anything not frequent is thrown away and never expanded, saving an enormous amount of computation.
  • The five steps: 1-itemsets → discard → 2-itemsets → discard → 3-itemsets → discard → stop when nothing passes → take the surviving frequent itemsets, generate rules, and filter by confidence.

The most important lesson in this example — read it with lift

Both rules hit 100% confidence, which looks excellent. But compute the lift:

Lift({Eggs} → {Milk})Confidence ÷ Support({Milk}) = 100% ÷ 100% = 1
Lift({Bread} → {Milk})100% ÷ 100% = 1

A lift of exactly 1 means there is no association at all. The reason: customers buy milk every time they buy eggs not because eggs and milk are related, but because they buy milk in every transaction regardless (Support({Milk}) = 100%). High confidence can deceive. Had milk's support been lower — say 50% — and the lift still come out above 1, that would indicate a genuinely non-coincidental relationship. This is why lift matters: support and confidence alone are not enough.

Confidence is not symmetric — but lift is Confidence(X→Y) ≠ Confidence(Y→X) because the numerators are identical but the denominators are not. Support(Milk ∧ Bread) and Support(Bread ∧ Milk) are the same value, since "and" commutes — but Support(Milk) and Support(Bread) are different. "Buying milk then bread" is not the same statement as "buying bread then milk". Lift, by contrast, is symmetric, because its denominator is Support(X) × Support(Y), which commutes.
6 · Generating rules from a 3-itemset — six of them

The hard rule: the union of antecedent and consequent must equal that itemset exactly, and both sides must be non-empty. So every item in the itemset must appear in the rule, on the left or the right, with none missing. From the itemset {Milk, Bread, Butter}:

3{Bread, Butter} → {Milk}Support(Milk,Bread,Butter) ÷ Support(Bread,Butter)

Notice the numerator is identical in all six — the support of the full 3-itemset. Only the denominator changes. The first three have a one-item consequent; the last three have a two-item consequent.

The common mistake From the 3-itemset {Milk, Bread, Butter} you cannot write {Milk} → {Bread}, because Butter is missing. That rule belongs to the 2-itemset {Milk, Bread}, not to this one.
May you stop at 2-itemsets? Yes. Stopping at 2-itemsets is perfectly valid even when larger itemsets exist — it simply means you are choosing to extract only pairwise relationships. If you need more complex bundles, go on to the 3-itemsets.

8 · A recipe for attacking one of these in the exam
  1. Write N first and circle it. Then write the given min support and min confidence beside it, and convert min support into a count straight away (60% of 5 = at least 3 occurrences) so you never recompute percentages.
  2. Count every 1-itemset, going item by item rather than transaction by transaction. Strike out anything that fails at once.
  3. Pair only the survivors into 2-itemsets. With k survivors you get k(k−1)/2 pairs (4 survivors = 6 pairs).
  4. Before counting any 3-itemset, check its subsets — if one of them is already dead, prune without counting. This is where the Apriori property saves you the most time.
  5. Stop when no itemset passes, then move to the rule stage.
  6. Generate rules from each surviving frequent itemset, one itemset at a time. A 2-itemset gives 2 rules; a 3-itemset gives 6. Remember that every item of that itemset must appear in the rule.
  7. Compute confidence with counts, not percentages — frequency of the whole set ÷ frequency of the left-hand side. The N cancels anyway.
  8. If the question also asks about lift, remember that high confidence with a lift of 1 is a useless rule — the point the slide is built to teach.
SET 4

Decision Trees

Class 5 Supervised, does both classification and regression, and is rule-based. The heart of it is which feature to ask first, answered by entropy + information gain — but the formulas are not examined, only the concept.

The other classifiers on the slide

KNN — predicts from the training points in the neighborhood of the test point.

Decision Tree — split at every node on a variable's value, then classify at a leaf from the aggregate class labels.

Naive Bayes — uses likelihood probabilities, assuming features are independent to simplify the model.

Logistic regression — fits class probabilities with a sigmoid, finding coefficients by gradient descent.

SVM — learns the hyperplane separating two classes with the maximum margin.

The slide's opening sentence can be asked as-is: "In classification models the target variable or class label is categorical, whereas in regression the target variable is numeric."

Entropy

Measures how messy or impure a group is.

Words an option may use instead: disorder · messy · impure · randomness · uncertainty.

Entropy = 0 → perfectly pure, everyone in the same category. The slide's example: reach into a bag of 10 Red Delicious apples, uncertainty 0.

Entropy = 1 → maximum mess, a perfect 50/50 split. A bag of 5 apples and 5 oranges — no idea what you will pull out.

His classroom analogy: everyone mixed = high entropy · men left, women right = low entropy · strictly alternating = maximum mess · a room of only women = entropy 0 at once.

Information Gain

Measures how much a question reduces that messiness.

IG = Entropy(Parent) − Weighted Average Entropy(Children)

It must be weighted, because each child holds a different number of data points — "this child gets more, that child gets less".

IG is positive and more is better → pick the feature with the highest IG as the question.

Short trees beat tall ones

A short tree finishes sooner, using less energy and computation → fewer questions is better.

His analogy: like queuing or seeing a doctor — there is a screening step, and the best screening separates people fastest. So the first question matters most.

The rule he repeated twice: an attribute used once may not be reused on the same path — "if you've used Outlook, you shouldn't have Outlook again further down".

The steps (ID3)

  • 1. Compute the entropy of the whole dataset.
  • 2. Try each feature as the root — split on each value, take each branch's entropy, then the weighted average.
  • 3. Compute IG for every feature and pick the highest.
  • 4. Draw the first split — root node and children.
  • 5. Recurse — repeat on every subset until a stopping condition is met.
  • The deck names ID3 by R. Quinlan, 1986 — but the name need not be memorised.

Stopping conditions and overfitting

  • Left alone the program does not stop — it splits until each leaf holds one point or none.
  • max depth — fix the number of levels in advance.
  • minimum samples — stop if fewer than n points remain (say < 50).
  • pure node — if entropy is 0 it becomes a leaf at once.
  • Why stop: going so deep that few points remain at the bottom is overfitting — "it has learned too much from the data we trained on".

Random Forest

  • A plain Decision Tree gives one tree — and one tree may not do well, like deciding alone.
  • Random Forest = 100 / 200 / 300 / 500 trees, then take the majority vote.
  • Each tree must be different — different feature-selection criteria, randomisation, a mix of tall and short. Diversity is the point.
  • He said himself: "we're not covering it, this basic one is enough for now".

In Orange

  • The ice-cream dataset: 12 data points, 4 features and 1 target, which must be marked explicitly (Orange flags it green, type categorical).
  • Induce binary tree: ticked forces binary; unticked branches on however many values exist.
  • The difference: with binary on, Weather's three values group Cloudy and Sunny together to be split later; with it off it fans into three branches at once. The trees differ, and neither is wrong.
  • Split subsets smaller than… is the stopping condition you can set.
SET 5

K-means Clustering

Class 5 Unsupervised and descriptive. He said outright: "this is an exam question — I might ask which category K-means falls into" and "I'm going to ask how a parameter and a hyperparameter differ".

Clustering ≠ Classification

Clustering groups similar data points without prior knowledge of the labels. Also called cluster analysis.

Classification is supervised and uses labels. Clustering is unsupervised and does not.

You cannot score clustering right or wrong — "you don't know what's right or wrong, you just group them". You can only ask whether it is good, by seeing whether it works when applied.

A real use: a product owner wants to know what kinds of people buy it, feeds in each person's attributes (in town / out of town, single, single mum …) and has them grouped — without knowing whether the answer is right.

K is a hyperparameter

K = the number of clusters you want, and the user chooses it in advance.

It is set by the user / data scientist, not learned by the model → so it is a hyperparameter, not a parameter.

"There's no right and no wrong" — Iris can be cut into 3, 4 or 5 groups. What matters is whether it works when applied.

Centroid and distance

A centroid is a cluster's centre point. Related: center, CG (center of gravity), COM (center of mass).

The centroid of a triangle is the intersection of its medians — the deck has this figure.

Similarity is measured by distance — closer means more alike. It uses Euclidean distance, straight from Pythagoras: d = √[(x₁−x₂)² + (y₁−y₂)²]

WCSS = Within-Cluster Sum of Squares — the squared distances from every point to its centroid, added up. Why square: it avoids a slow square root, and we only need to compare near from far, not a precise number → very fast to compute.

Normalization

The problem is unequal scales — age spans about 100, income nearly a million. In imports-85: horsepower 48–288 but price 5,000–40,005.

ML computes an error and squares it — a variable in the tens of thousands squares into the millions, one in the hundreds only into the tens of thousands → unfair, and the model over-weights the large-valued feature.

Normalization squeezes into 0 to 1. Standardization (Z-score) uses the standard deviation.

He tied it straight to the exam: "that's bias, and I ask about bias on the paper" → unequal scales count as data bias. And in Orange, normalize the features only, never the target.

Choosing k — the elbow method

  • The weaker way: guess from statistics or domain knowledge that there are about 5 groups. He said himself: "that isn't a good answer, it's trial and error".
  • The elbow method: try k = 1, 2, 3, … onwards (to 10, say).
  • For each k compute WCSS (the errors of all clusters added together), then plot it against k.
  • Take the point where the error starts to flatten — where the slope goes level. That is the elbow.
  • The deck's example: "3 at best or 4 if needed". Elbow means the joint of the arm.
  • There is also the Silhouette Score — the deck names it, but he said "not for now".

K-modes and K-prototypes

  • K-means is not suitable for categorical data — the deck says even one-hot encoding leaves Euclidean distance misleading.
  • K-means → numerical → Euclidean distance → uses the mean.
  • K-modes → categorical → counts mismatches (like Hamming distance) → uses the mode.
  • Same = mismatch 0, different = mismatch 1. Fewer mismatches means closer. New York vs New York = 0; New York vs Chicago = 1.
  • K-prototypes combines both. Its centre is a prototype because it is the most "typical" point of the cluster. cost = (age₁−age₂)² + (income₁−income₂)² + (number of mismatches)

Hard vs soft clustering

  • Hard clustering — a sample goes to exactly one cluster. Example: K-means.
  • Fuzzy / soft clustering — a sample is distributed among several clusters by weight. Example: Fuzzy C-means (FCM).
  • The deck's apple: an apple is red or green (hard) — but it can also be red and green (fuzzy), red to a degree and green to a degree.
  • He put it as a point being 80% in one cluster and 20% in another.
  • Why C and not K: C stands for class / category — the number of centres. Same meaning as K.
