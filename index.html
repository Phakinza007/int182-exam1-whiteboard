SET 1

Intro to Data Science, Engineering and AI

Classes 1–2 The set that lays down the whole vocabulary: who does what, what big data is, how AI/ML/DL/DS nest, and where KDD and data mining differ. He said this set "has quite a few questions on it".

People and roles

  • Data science — the term was proposed by Peter Naur in 1974 as an alternative name for computer science.
  • Data scientist — coined by DJ Patil and Jeff Hammerbacher in 2008.
  • "A jack of all trades and a master of some" — like a duck, does many things and is expert at a few. The skills: statistics + computing + domain knowledge.
  • The 4 A's of the role — Architecture, Acquisition, Analysis, Archiving.
  • Data engineering is upstream — infrastructure, collecting and storing, ETL (Extract, Transform, Load).

The two quotes

  • "Data is the new oil" — Clive Humby, 2006. Valuable, but useless unrefined: it must be broken down and analysed, like crude oil.
  • "AI is the new electricity" — Andrew Ng, 2016. AI will transform industry the way electricity once did, and everyone can reach it.
  • Democratize AI — a phrase Jensen Huang of NVIDIA uses often: everyone should be able to reach AI, not just an elite.
  • AI winter — a stretch where activity and investment go quiet, the downward part of the hype cycle. We are on the way up right now.

Big Data

  • The 5 V's — Volume · Velocity, the speed it arrives · Variety of types and sources · Veracity, quality and trustworthiness · Value for decision-making.
  • The slide names Value the most important of the five.
  • Plus 2 — Variability, meaning that shifts with context, and Visualization.
  • Structured has a clear shape, like tables in a relational database. Unstructured has none — text, images, audio, video.
  • A figure worth citing: in 2025 the world produced roughly 400 quintillion bytes of new data per day (a quintillion is 10¹⁸).

Pipeline and life cycle

In practice data science is the whole pipeline, not just the modelling step.

Two facts that can be asked directly: the longest step is data preparation, and the most important step is EDA.

The data lifecycle parallels the SDLC — he said "I ask about this one".

KDD and data mining

KDD = Knowledge Discovery in Databases — the whole process of discovering knowledge from databases.

Data mining is one step inside KDD — pulling previously unknown information and patterns out of large databases.

A data warehouse is divided by time period, which is what makes historical comparison possible.

Insight → actionable

A data product is the output of a data science activity — a recommender system, a model, or the dataset used to train it.

An insight is a deep understanding arrived at by analysing data.

Actionable is the property that lets you act on it — do X, and Y improves.

Three kinds of bias

Bias is error that creeps in, sometimes unintentionally, out of unnoticed habit.

Data bias — in the data itself, e.g. unequal scales making the model weight things wrongly. Human bias. Algorithmic bias — arising from training.

He said he asks "what kinds of bias are there" and "what do you do to reduce bias".

This course's tools

Orange Data Mining (orangedatamining.com) — visual programming, dragging widgets and wiring them. A workspace file is .ows.

RStudio (posit.co). Datasets from UCI and Kaggle.

He said "there's one question on Orange — should be easy, a free mark".

Words that get mixed up

Feature engineering = using domain knowledge to extract new variables from raw data. Feature selection = choosing only the subset that genuinely matters.

An instance is one row of data — also called a data point, row, record or observation.

Data imputation = substituting a value for missing data, for instance the mean or the most frequent value.

SET 2

EDA and Model Fitting

Class 3 The set with the most confusable definitions — and the one he named the most topics from: box plot, scatter plot, EDA, predictive vs descriptive, parameter vs hyperparameter, OLTP/OLAP, data types.

What EDA is

Exploratory Data Analysis = a technique for understanding your data by summarizing its main characteristics, usually through statistical graphics and visualization.

EDA is non-parametric — it makes no assumptions about the underlying distribution.

It takes in data visualization + statistical summaries + hypothesis testing, and is iterative — not done once and finished.

The trap that could show up: a box plot is EDA, but linear regression is not — it is data modeling. The slide says so outright.

How many variables at once

Univariate = 1 variable — a box plot (one axis).

Bivariate = 2 — a scatter plot (x and y).

Multivariate = 3 or more — predicting price from horsepower + engine size + brand.

The four confusable words

Modeling (broadest) — creating a simplified representation of a system or process: equations, simulations, or a physical model.

Data modeling — a conceptual representation of data, to understand it, communicate it and build systems on it. Includes ERD, DFD, UML.

Data fitting — finding the mathematical function that best describes the existing data. Techniques: least squares regression, polynomial fitting, spline interpolation.

Model fitting — focused on the model itself, tuning the parameters of a pre-selected model. The emphasis is generalization — working on unseen data. Techniques: gradient descent, maximum likelihood.

The nesting: Modeling ⊃ Data modeling ⊃ (Data fitting, Model fitting).

Parameter vs hyperparameter

A model parameter is estimated from the data by the algorithm itself — weights, coefficients.

A model hyperparameter is set manually by a person, used in the process of estimating the parameters — k in KNN, k in K-means, learning rate, number of layers, epochs, the loss function.

This is where open weight model, heard so often around generative AI, comes from. A straight line needs only a slope and an intercept; a modern model holds billions or trillions of parameters.

Underfitting vs overfitting

Underfitting — the model is too simple to capture the underlying patterns, or there is too little data or too little training.

Overfitting — the model is too complex and learns the noise as well: excellent on training data, poor on new data.

Noise is the part of the data that arrives unwanted and serves no purpose, but which the model learns anyway. The fix for overfitting is validation.

His tailoring analogy: overfitting is a suit cut exactly for one person that fits nobody else — not general. Underfitting is a suit that is too loose.

Predictive vs descriptive

The models produced by the data mining step fall into two types.

Predictive — predict one variable's value from the rest. The one predicted is the dependent or target variable; the rest are independent or predictor variables.

Descriptive — identify the relationships between variables in order to learn about the structure of the data. It does not predict.

Example: "this make, a sedan, this much horsepower, black — roughly what price?" is predictive; a descriptive model simply reports what it found. Clustering and association rules are descriptive; classification and regression are predictive.

Types of data and scales of measurement — "this one matters, we talk about it constantly"

What a true zero is A true zero means zero really is the complete absence of the quantity — 0 kg is no weight, 0 baht is no money, 0 K is no thermal energy. But 0 °C is not, because heat is still present; it is a number human beings agreed on.
The consequence 50 °C is not twice as hot as 25 °C, but 100 K is twice 50 K. AD years have no true zero either, so the year 2000 is not "twice" the year 1000. Nor does a TOEFL score — answering everything wrong still leaves a baseline.
An athlete's shirt number 8, 10, 23 on a shirt are numbers, but they are only symbols and say nothing about who is better — so they are nominal, not numerical. Seeing digits is not enough; consider the purpose and context.

What a box plot is for — asked directly

Dispersion (spread) · skewness, if any · the quartiles · the outliers, if any · and it compares groups side by side.

Its advantage over a histogram: you see the distribution without plotting everything, and several groups fit side by side in one picture.

What a scatter plot is for — asked directly

It plots two variables against each other (bivariate) to reveal the relationship between them — moving together, moving oppositely, or not related at all.

He also asks "why do we plot at all?" — because you cannot see a pattern in a table of numbers, but you see it at once in a plot.

The three measures of centre

Mean — the familiar average. It takes every data point into account but is pulled by outliers.

Median — the middle value once sorted = Q2.

Mode — the most common value = the peak of a histogram (its y axis is frequency).

The slide stresses that no single one is best, but using just one is a bad idea.

The shape of a distribution

Normal — symmetric, bell-shaped. Mean, median and mode all land on the same point.

Positive skew — extreme values on the right drag the values right. Negative skew — dragged left.

The mean is always pulled towards the tail, with the median between the mode and the mean. Most people are 1.7–1.8 m tall, so a few over 2 m drag the average right.

Correlation

Runs from −1 to 1. 0 = no correlation. Positive = same direction; negative = opposite.

Pearson for numeric, linear relationships. Spearman for ordinal. This course uses Pearson.

Correlation does not mean causation — a sentence that can be asked as-is. And the formula is not examined.

Linear regression and reading the output

Choose the line with the smallest total squared error, by least squares. Of the hundreds of lines that could pass through the data, one is best.

"Regression" translates as "receding" — the error recedes until it is as small as it goes.

The constraint: y must always be numeric, while x may mix numeric and categorical.

The stars come from the t-test; more is better (p < 0.05 counts as significant). No stars at all = that variable is not important enough to predict with.

The result from class: Price = −4562.175 + 172.206 × Horsepower, with r = 0.81.

SET 3

Association Rules — Market Basket Analysis

Class 4 + the class 5 refill The only set with a formula on the paper. It is unsupervised and descriptive, and rule-based like a decision tree.

The three formulas

He stressed that you must be able to write support and confidence yourself — the paper will not supply them. Lift can just be read through.

Support(X)= transactions containing X ÷ total transactions. It answers "how often does this item, or this set, occur?"
Confidence(X→Y)= Support(X,Y) ÷ Support(X), from conditional probability. It answers "given X is in the basket, how confident are we that Y is too?"
Lift(X→Y)= Confidence(X→Y) ÷ Support(Y) = Support(X,Y) ÷ [Support(X) × Support(Y)]

Vocabulary

  • An association rule is an if–then rule written X → Y, derived from transaction data. Also called association mining.
  • X on the left is the antecedent; Y on the right is the consequent. A comma means "and".
  • MBA = Market Basket Analysis — finding what gets bought together. The classic example is beer & diapers.
  • Transactional data records one event and should carry a timestamp — but MBA ignores quantities and order.
  • An itemset is a set, so it has no duplicates and no order.

Apriori

  • Part 1: if an itemset is frequent → all of its subsets are frequent.
  • Part 2: if an itemset is infrequent → all of its supersets are infrequent (the contrapositive of part 1).
  • Why pruning matters: the power set has size 2ⁿ. With thousands of products that is uncomputable, so infrequent itemsets are cut immediately.
  • The steps: 1-itemsets → discard → 2-itemsets → discard → 3-itemsets → discard → stop when nothing survives.
  • In practice 3–4 itemsets is about right; past 5 the patterns get hard to find and stop being useful.

Watch out

  • A 1-itemset cannot make a rule — you need at least 2 items, since each side of the arrow needs at least one. Neither side may be empty.
  • Confidence is not symmetric — Confidence(X→Y) ≠ Confidence(Y→X), because the numerators match but the denominators do not.
  • The high-confidence trap: a slide example reaches 100% confidence but a lift of exactly 1, because milk is in every transaction anyway → always check lift too.
  • Reading lift: > 1 a real association · = 1 independent · < 1 a negative association. Slide 6 mistypes this.
  • A 3-itemset gives 6 rules — three with a one-item consequent and three with a two-item consequent. Every item must appear; none may be missing.

The two worked examples

The slide examplemin sup 50%, min conf 75% → the surviving strong rules are {Bread}→{Milk} and {Eggs}→{Milk}, both at 100% confidence — but both with lift 1, which is the lesson of the example.
The 5-transaction exercisemin sup 60%, min conf 80% → one strong rule survives: {Butter}→{Bread}, confidence 100%, lift 1.25.
SET 4

Decision Trees

Class 5 Supervised, does both classification and regression, and is rule-based. The heart of it is which feature to ask first, answered by entropy + information gain — but the formulas are not examined, only the concept.

The other classifiers on the slide

KNN — predicts from the training points in the neighborhood of the test point.

Decision Tree — split at every node on a variable's value, then classify at a leaf from the aggregate class labels.

Naive Bayes — uses likelihood probabilities, assuming features are independent to simplify the model.

Logistic regression — fits class probabilities with a sigmoid, finding coefficients by gradient descent.

SVM — learns the hyperplane separating two classes with the maximum margin.

The slide's opening sentence can be asked as-is: "In classification models the target variable or class label is categorical, whereas in regression the target variable is numeric."

Entropy

Measures how messy or impure a group is.

Words an option may use instead: disorder · messy · impure · randomness · uncertainty.

Entropy = 0 → perfectly pure, everyone in the same category. The slide's example: reach into a bag of 10 Red Delicious apples, uncertainty 0.

Entropy = 1 → maximum mess, a perfect 50/50 split. A bag of 5 apples and 5 oranges — no idea what you will pull out.

His classroom analogy: everyone mixed = high entropy · men left, women right = low entropy · strictly alternating = maximum mess · a room of only women = entropy 0 at once.

Information Gain

Measures how much a question reduces that messiness.

IG = Entropy(Parent) − Weighted Average Entropy(Children)

It must be weighted, because each child holds a different number of data points — "this child gets more, that child gets less".

IG is positive and more is better → pick the feature with the highest IG as the question.

Short trees beat tall ones

A short tree finishes sooner, using less energy and computation → fewer questions is better.

His analogy: like queuing or seeing a doctor — there is a screening step, and the best screening separates people fastest. So the first question matters most.

The rule he repeated twice: an attribute used once may not be reused on the same path — "if you've used Outlook, you shouldn't have Outlook again further down".

The steps (ID3)

  • 1. Compute the entropy of the whole dataset.
  • 2. Try each feature as the root — split on each value, take each branch's entropy, then the weighted average.
  • 3. Compute IG for every feature and pick the highest.
  • 4. Draw the first split — root node and children.
  • 5. Recurse — repeat on every subset until a stopping condition is met.
  • The deck names ID3 by R. Quinlan, 1986 — but the name need not be memorised.

Stopping conditions and overfitting

  • Left alone the program does not stop — it splits until each leaf holds one point or none.
  • max depth — fix the number of levels in advance.
  • minimum samples — stop if fewer than n points remain (say < 50).
  • pure node — if entropy is 0 it becomes a leaf at once.
  • Why stop: going so deep that few points remain at the bottom is overfitting — "it has learned too much from the data we trained on".

Random Forest

  • A plain Decision Tree gives one tree — and one tree may not do well, like deciding alone.
  • Random Forest = 100 / 200 / 300 / 500 trees, then take the majority vote.
  • Each tree must be different — different feature-selection criteria, randomisation, a mix of tall and short. Diversity is the point.
  • He said himself: "we're not covering it, this basic one is enough for now".

In Orange

  • The ice-cream dataset: 12 data points, 4 features and 1 target, which must be marked explicitly (Orange flags it green, type categorical).
  • Induce binary tree: ticked forces binary; unticked branches on however many values exist.
  • The difference: with binary on, Weather's three values group Cloudy and Sunny together to be split later; with it off it fans into three branches at once. The trees differ, and neither is wrong.
  • Split subsets smaller than… is the stopping condition you can set.
SET 5

K-means Clustering

Class 5 Unsupervised and descriptive. He said outright: "this is an exam question — I might ask which category K-means falls into" and "I'm going to ask how a parameter and a hyperparameter differ".

Clustering ≠ Classification

Clustering groups similar data points without prior knowledge of the labels. Also called cluster analysis.

Classification is supervised and uses labels. Clustering is unsupervised and does not.

You cannot score clustering right or wrong — "you don't know what's right or wrong, you just group them". You can only ask whether it is good, by seeing whether it works when applied.

A real use: a product owner wants to know what kinds of people buy it, feeds in each person's attributes (in town / out of town, single, single mum …) and has them grouped — without knowing whether the answer is right.

K is a hyperparameter

K = the number of clusters you want, and the user chooses it in advance.

It is set by the user / data scientist, not learned by the model → so it is a hyperparameter, not a parameter.

"There's no right and no wrong" — Iris can be cut into 3, 4 or 5 groups. What matters is whether it works when applied.

Centroid and distance

A centroid is a cluster's centre point. Related: center, CG (center of gravity), COM (center of mass).

The centroid of a triangle is the intersection of its medians — the deck has this figure.

Similarity is measured by distance — closer means more alike. It uses Euclidean distance, straight from Pythagoras: d = √[(x₁−x₂)² + (y₁−y₂)²]

WCSS = Within-Cluster Sum of Squares — the squared distances from every point to its centroid, added up. Why square: it avoids a slow square root, and we only need to compare near from far, not a precise number → very fast to compute.

Normalization

The problem is unequal scales — age spans about 100, income nearly a million. In imports-85: horsepower 48–288 but price 5,000–40,005.

ML computes an error and squares it — a variable in the tens of thousands squares into the millions, one in the hundreds only into the tens of thousands → unfair, and the model over-weights the large-valued feature.

Normalization squeezes into 0 to 1. Standardization (Z-score) uses the standard deviation.

He tied it straight to the exam: "that's bias, and I ask about bias on the paper" → unequal scales count as data bias. And in Orange, normalize the features only, never the target.

Choosing k — the elbow method

  • The weaker way: guess from statistics or domain knowledge that there are about 5 groups. He said himself: "that isn't a good answer, it's trial and error".
  • The elbow method: try k = 1, 2, 3, … onwards (to 10, say).
  • For each k compute WCSS (the errors of all clusters added together), then plot it against k.
  • Take the point where the error starts to flatten — where the slope goes level. That is the elbow.
  • The deck's example: "3 at best or 4 if needed". Elbow means the joint of the arm.
  • There is also the Silhouette Score — the deck names it, but he said "not for now".

K-modes and K-prototypes

  • K-means is not suitable for categorical data — the deck says even one-hot encoding leaves Euclidean distance misleading.
  • K-means → numerical → Euclidean distance → uses the mean.
  • K-modes → categorical → counts mismatches (like Hamming distance) → uses the mode.
  • Same = mismatch 0, different = mismatch 1. Fewer mismatches means closer. New York vs New York = 0; New York vs Chicago = 1.
  • K-prototypes combines both. Its centre is a prototype because it is the most "typical" point of the cluster. cost = (age₁−age₂)² + (income₁−income₂)² + (number of mismatches)

Hard vs soft clustering

  • Hard clustering — a sample goes to exactly one cluster. Example: K-means.
  • Fuzzy / soft clustering — a sample is distributed among several clusters by weight. Example: Fuzzy C-means (FCM).
  • The deck's apple: an apple is red or green (hard) — but it can also be red and green (fuzzy), red to a degree and green to a degree.
  • He put it as a point being 80% in one cluster and 20% in another.
  • Why C and not K: C stands for class / category — the number of centres. Same meaning as K.

From the EDA – Model Fitting deck
?What is a box plot for? — "I ask about the box plot directly"It shows dispersion, skewness, the quartiles and the outliers — and it compares groups. Its advantage over a histogram is seeing the distribution without plotting everything, and fitting several groups side by side.
?What is a scatter plot for, and why plot at all? — "go and find the answer yourselves"It plots two variables against each other (bivariate) to show the relationship — together, opposite, or none. You plot because a table of numbers hides the pattern that a plot reveals instantly.
?Define EDA — "of course I ask about EDA, you must be able to define it"A technique for understanding your data by summarizing its main characteristics, usually through statistical graphics and visualization. Non-parametric — no assumptions about the distribution. It is visualization + statistical summary + hypothesis testing, done iteratively.
?How do predictive and descriptive differ? — "you have to be able to explain it"Predictive predicts one variable from the rest — the predicted one is the target/dependent, the others are predictors/independent. Descriptive finds relationships to understand the data's structure, without predicting. Classification and regression are predictive; clustering and association rules are descriptive.
?How do a model parameter and a hyperparameter differ? — "there'll be options to pick from"A parameter is estimated from the data by the algorithm — weights, coefficients. A hyperparameter is set by a person — k in K-means, k in KNN, learning rate, layers, epochs, the loss function.
?How do model fitting, data fitting and data modeling differ?They nest: Modeling ⊃ Data modeling ⊃ (Data fitting, Model fitting). Data fitting finds the function best describing existing data (least squares, polynomial fitting, splines). Model fitting tunes a pre-selected model's parameters, aiming at generalization (gradient descent, maximum likelihood).
?OLTP vs OLAP — "if I remember rightly I ask about these"OLTP = Online Transaction Processing — day-to-day transactions, small frequent reads and writes. OLAP = Online Analytical Processing — analysis over large historical reads, on top of a data warehouse.
?What is ratio, what is interval? — "I will ask"Ratio has a true zero (weight, length, pulse, Kelvin), so "how many times" is meaningful. Interval has none (°C, °F, pH, AD year, TOEFL score): differences mean something, ratios do not. 50 °C is not twice 25 °C, but 100 K is twice 50 K.
?Q1, Q2, Q3, the IQR, the fences and outliers — "this you have to understand"IQR = Q3 − Q1, the width of the box. Fences at Q1 − 1.5×IQR and Q3 + 1.5×IQR (some use 3, but 1.5 is more common). Outside a fence is an outlier. min/max count only points inside. The five-number summary is min, Q1, Q2, Q3, max.
?Correlation — no formula neededIt runs −1 to 1. 0 = none. Positive = same direction, negative = opposite. Pearson for numeric and linear, Spearman for ordinal. Correlation is not causation.
?How do you read the coefficients and the stars?The stars come from the t-test; more is better (p < 0.05 counts as significant). No stars at all means that variable is not important enough to predict with. p-value and R² are not asked.
?Which category does K-means fall into? — "this is an exam question"Unsupervised learning. The reason: no label is used. It sits separately from classification and regression in the AI diagram.
?How is support computed? — "if it comes up, it's one"Support(X) = transactions containing X ÷ total transactions. And memorise Confidence(X→Y) = Support(X,Y) ÷ Support(X) too.
?What is reinforcement learning?Learning by trial and error with a reward — points for doing well, a penalty for doing badly. Examples: chess or Go, a robot learning to walk. In Gen AI it is the 👍👎 buttons, collected and fed back.

The traps the options will use

He said the hard part is that "the options sit close together". Every pair below is close enough to be swapped.
A box plot is EDA; linear regression is notThe slide says so outright. Linear regression is data modeling, not EDA — though EDA finds the patterns that then feed a regression model.
Value, not VolumeThe slide names Value the most important V, not Volume — and Volume is the tempting option because it sounds more like "big" data.
DS vs DE, not DS vs DBAThe Data Engineer is upstream (infrastructure, storage, ETL); the Data Scientist is downstream (explore, analyse, model, interpret). Do not swap them.
KDD ⊃ data mining, not the reverseKDD is the whole process and data mining is one step inside it — an option will reverse it and make data mining the larger.
Interval vs ratio — look for the true zero°C and °F are interval, because 0 degrees is not the absence of heat. Kelvin is ratio. AD years and test scores are interval. Weight, length and pulse are ratio.
A shirt number is nominal8, 10, 23 are numbers, but only symbols — they say nothing about who is better → nominal, not numerical. Digits alone are not enough; consider the purpose.
The mode is the histogram's peakA histogram's y axis is frequency, so the peak is the most common value = the mode. The mean is pulled towards the tail, and the median = Q2 sits between them.
Whiskers end on data, not on the fenceA whisker runs to the furthest real data point still inside the fence → the two need not be equal. And min/max exclude outliers.
Confidence is not symmetricConfidence(X→Y) ≠ Confidence(Y→X) — the numerators match but the denominators do not. Support is symmetric, since Support(X,Y) = Support(Y,X).
100% confidence does not mean usefulA slide example hits 100% confidence with a lift of exactly 1, because milk is in every transaction anyway — the rule tells you nothing new. Always check lift too.
A Decision Tree does regression tooAn option will claim DT does classification only. Wrong — the deck has a "Predict a house price" example with a numeric label. That is its advantage.
Entropy 1 is not "good"0 = pure, the best. 1 = maximum mess. Close to 1 means still thoroughly mixed. PlayTennis starts at 0.94 — not orderly at all.
Pick the highest IG, not the lowestMore IG is better, because it means more mess removed. In the example IG(Outlook) = 0.247 beats Humidity's 0.15, so Outlook is the root.
K-means does not repeat itselfIt is non-deterministic — repeat runs on the same data differ, because the starting centroids are random. It is also sensitive to initial placement and can converge to a poor solution.
Clustering has no right answerWith no labels there is nothing to be right or wrong against. You can only ask whether it is good in use. Three groups or four — neither is wrong.
K-means cannot take categorical dataEven encoded numerically with one-hot, Euclidean distance stays misleading → use K-modes (mismatch counts) or K-prototypes for mixed data.
Normalize the features onlyNever the target, because the target is the output. Skipping it entirely makes the model lean towards large-valued features — which counts as data bias.
y must always be numericIn linear regression the dependent variable y must be numeric, while x may mix numeric and categorical — an option will swap the two.

  • 65
  • 75
  • Both 65 and 75

Which is true of a box plot's whiskers?

tap for the answer
A whisker must end on an observed data point — the furthest from the box that is still inside the fence, not at the fence itself. So the two need not match, since the furthest inside point differs on each side. Outliers lie beyond the fence and are plotted as separate points.

A rule {Bread} → {Milk} has 100% confidence but a lift of exactly 1. What does that mean?

  • Bread makes Milk less likely
  • The calculation is wrong, since 100% confidence must give a high lift
tap for the answer
A lift of 1 means independence — the rule tells you nothing new. Confidence reaches 100% only because milk is in every transaction anyway, with or without bread. This is the most important lesson in the slide's example: high confidence alone is not enough, always check lift. Reading lift: >1 a real association · =1 independent · <1 negative.
How many association rules can be generated from one 3-itemset?
  • 3
  • 4
  • 6
  • 8

Six — three with a one-item consequent (e.g. {A,B}→{C}) and three with a two-item consequent (e.g. {A}→{B,C}). The hard rule: every item must appear, none may be dropped, and each side of the arrow needs at least one.
Which statement is the Apriori property?
  • If an itemset is infrequent, all of its supersets are infrequent
  • If an itemset is infrequent, all of its subsets are infrequent
  • If an itemset is frequent, all of its supersets are frequent
  • All itemsets of the same size have the same support
tap for the answer
There are two parts: (1) frequent → all subsets frequent, and (2) infrequent → all supersets infrequent, the contrapositive of the first. The second is what makes pruning possible — the power set has size 2ⁿ, so without cutting early, thousands of products are uncomputable. The other options swap subset and superset.
